
1. OVERVIEW / INTRODUCTION
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Leaf Reddening in cotton is a PHYSIOLOGICAL DISORDER rather than a single disease 
caused by a specific pathogen. It is a symptom complex in which cotton leaves turn 
red, reddish-purple, or bronze, caused by accumulation of anthocyanin pigments in 
leaf cells. This happens when normal physiological processes are disrupted.

Leaf Reddening is one of the most common late-season problems in cotton fields 
across Pakistan (especially Punjab) and '' (Punjab, Haryana, Maharashtra), often 
mistaken for disease infection when it is primarily a stress response.

IMPORTANT DISTINCTION:
  - "True" Leaf Reddening = physiological stress (nutrition, water, temperature)
  - "Pest-induced" Reddening = caused by mite infestation (spider mites/red mites)
  - "Pathogen-induced" Reddening = caused by Cotton Leaf Curl Virus (CLCuV) 
    or Fusarium wilt in advanced stage
  
This document focuses on the most common causes: PHYSIOLOGICAL (prevention-focused).

Economic Impact:
  - Premature leaf reddening indicates stressed plants = reduced photosynthesis
  - If reddening occurs before boll-set, yield loss can reach 20–35%
  - Post boll-set reddening (natural senescence) = normal, minimal yield impact
  - Pakistan: Late-season reddening reduces fiber quality (staple length, micronaire)

2. CAUSES OF LEAF REDDENING IN COTTON
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

2.1 Magnesium (Mg) Deficiency — MOST COMMON CAUSE in Pakistan/''
----------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Magnesium is the central atom of chlorophyll molecule
  - Deficiency: Chlorophyll breaks down → anthocyanins become visible → RED color
  - Reddening pattern: INTERVEINAL (between leaf veins) — veins remain green
  - Starts in: Older (lower) leaves first, progresses upward
  - Pakistan soils: Sandy loam soils in Multan, D.G. Khan lose Mg rapidly
  - '': Extensive irrigation in Haryana/Punjab leaches Mg from sandy soils

2.2 Potassium (K) Deficiency
------------------------------
  - K deficiency causes reddening at LEAF MARGINS and TIPS (not interveinal)
  - Older leaves affected first
  - Combined K+Mg deficiency = widespread reddening of entire leaf
  - Very common in light-textured soils with high irrigation frequency

2.3 Water Stress (Drought or Waterlogging)
--------------------------------------------
  A. Drought Stress:
     - Insufficient water at critical growth stages (flowering, boll development)
     - Leaves accumulate carbohydrates → anthocyanin production increases → RED
     - Drought reddening: Uniform, whole-plant response starting from older leaves
  
  B. Waterlogging:
     - Roots deprived of oxygen → cannot absorb nutrients (especially K and Mg)
     - Leads to same nutrient deficiency symptoms (reddening) from wet conditions
     - Pakistan: Low-lying fields, tail ends of irrigation channels = high risk

2.4 Temperature Stress
------------------------
  - Cold nights (below 15°C): Slows enzyme activity, disrupts nutrient transport
  - Carbohydrate buildup → anthocyanin formation → REDDENING
  - Pakistan/'': September–October nights cool rapidly → late-season reddening
  - NOT necessarily damaging if bolls are already set

2.5 Pest-Induced Reddening — Spider Mites / Red Mites
-------------------------------------------------------
  - Tetranychus urticae (Two-spotted spider mite) and Tetranychus cinnabarinus 
    (Red spider mite) cause bronze/reddish stippling on leaves
  - Mites feed on leaf cells → cell destruction → red/bronze discoloration
  - Distinguishable by: Fine webbing on leaf undersides, tiny moving dots
  - Pakistan: Massive problem in July–September during hot dry weather
  - '': Widespread in Vidarbha, Punjab cotton during water-stressed periods

2.6 Cotton Leaf Curl Virus (CLCuV) — Secondary Reddening
-----------------------------------------------------------
  - CLCuV causes leaf curling, vein darkening, and in advanced stages — reddening
  - Spread by whitefly (Bemisia tabaci)
  - SERIOUS VIRAL DISEASE — separate management required (see related document)
  - Differentiate: CLCuV shows curling + reddening + vein swelling
  - Physiological reddening: NO curling, NO vein swelling, NO enations

2.7 Fusarium Wilt / Root Rot
------------------------------
  - Fungal infection of roots → disrupts water/nutrient transport → leaf red
  - Accompanied by: Wilting, yellowing, stem browning at base
  - Less common cause of reddening alone

3. PREVENTION STRATEGIES — DETAILED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

3.1 SOIL TESTING & BALANCED FERTILIZATION
------------------------------------------
THE MOST EFFECTIVE PREVENTION STRATEGY

  Pre-season soil testing (before planting) for:
    - Available Magnesium (target: >120 mg/kg soil)
    - Exchangeable Potassium (target: >160 mg/kg soil)
    - Soil pH (optimal for cotton: 6.5–7.5)
    - Organic matter content

  Pakistan: Soil testing labs at:
    - AARI Faisalabad
    - Soil & Water Testing Laboratories, Agriculture Departments in all districts
    - Cost: PKR 500–1500 per sample for complete analysis

  '': Soil Health Card Scheme — government soil testing (free or subsidized)
    - Apply online: soilhealth.dac.gov.in

  MAGNESIUM APPLICATION:
    - Soil application: Magnesium Sulfate (Kieserite) @ 10–15 kg/acre 
      before planting or at flower initiation stage
    - Foliar spray: Magnesium Sulfate (MgSO4) 2% solution
      (20 g per liter of water) spray 2–3 times at 15-day intervals
    - Best timing: When first signs of interveinal chlorosis appear (before red)

  POTASSIUM APPLICATION:
    - Muriate of Potash (MOP — 60% K2O): 25–30 kg/acre in two splits
      * 1st split: At planting (mix in furrow)
      * 2nd split: At first square/flower bud stage
    - Sulfate of Potash (SOP): Preferred in saline soils — does not add chloride
    - Foliar potassium: Potassium Nitrate (KNO3) 2% spray during peak demand

3.2 PROPER IRRIGATION MANAGEMENT
-----------------------------------
  Cotton water requirements by growth stage:
    Stage 1 (Germination–30 days): 30–50 mm/irrigation interval 7–10 days
    Stage 2 (Vegetative 30–60 days): 50–70 mm/irrigation interval 7–10 days
    Stage 3 (Flowering 60–90 days): CRITICAL — 70–80 mm, never let wilt occur
    Stage 4 (Boll development): 60–70 mm, maintain until boll opening begins

  Pakistan (Canal Irrigation System):
    - Coordinate with warabandi (water turn) schedule for stage-3 water needs
    - Do not skip irrigation in July–August flowering period — triggers reddening
    - Use soil moisture check: Insert finger 10 cm into soil — if dry, irrigate

  '' (Tube well/Drip systems):
    - Drip irrigation at 0.7 × Evapotranspiration rate minimizes moisture stress
    - Install tensiometers at 20 and 40 cm depth to monitor soil moisture tension
    - Irrigate when tensiometer reading > 40 centibars (field gets dry)

  AVOID WATERLOGGING:
    - Prepare proper drainage channels (khal/nala) before planting
    - Raise bed planting in waterlogging-prone areas (Pakistan Sindh low areas)
    - Do not flood-irrigate during afternoon heat — causes temporary waterlogging
    - Furrow irrigation is better than flood irrigation for drainage management

3.3 ORGANIC MATTER MANAGEMENT
--------------------------------
  - Incorporate crop residues/compost 3–4 weeks before planting
  - Apply farmyard manure (FYM): 4–5 tonnes/acre (Pakistan) / 5 tonnes/acre ('')
  - Organic matter improves:
    * Magnesium and potassium availability (reduces deficiency risk)
    * Water holding capacity (reduces drought stress and reddening)
    * Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC) — holds nutrients in soil longer
  - Green manure: Grow dhaincha (Sesbania bispinosa) and plow under 45 days 
    before cotton planting — excellent for Mg and micronutrient supply

3.4 pH MANAGEMENT
------------------
  - Very high pH soils (>8.0): Common in Pakistan's canal command areas
  - High pH LOCKS UP Magnesium, Zinc, Iron — plant cannot absorb them 
    even if present in soil → reddening from deficiency
  - Acidify soil with: Sulfur (ganda) @ 5–10 kg/acre worked into top 15 cm
  - Use Ammonium Sulfate (instead of Urea) as nitrogen source — slightly acidifying
  - Gypsum (calcium sulfate) @ 100–200 kg/acre in sodic soils
  - Pakistan Sindh saline-sodic soils need gypsum + sulfur treatment first

3.5 SPIDER MITE PREVENTION (Pest-Induced Reddening)
------------------------------------------------------
  - Spider mites thrive in HOT, DRY, DUSTY conditions
  - PREVENTION before mites establish:
    A. Water sprays on plant foliage (plain water mist) in early morning 
       — disrupts mite colony establishment and washes off eggs
    B. Keep field edges and roads moist to reduce dust
    C. Conserve predatory mites: Phytoseiidae (natural mite predators)
       — do not kill with broad-spectrum pesticides
    D. Predatory mites (Phytoseiulus persimilis, Neoseiulus californicus) 
       are available commercially — release @ 10–20 per plant at first sign
    E. Apply wettable sulfur (0.3%) spray at 2-week intervals preventively 
       during July–August hot period — kills mite eggs and young mites
    F. Acaricide rotation: If mites appear, rotate:
       Abamectin → Spiromesifen → Hexythiazox to prevent resistance

3.6 WHITEFLY CONTROL TO PREVENT CLCuV REDDENING
-------------------------------------------------
  - Whitefly (Bemisia tabaci) is vector of CLCuV
  - Prevent whitefly buildup to prevent virus transmission:
    A. Yellow sticky traps @ 8–10/acre for monitoring
    B. Reflective mulch to confuse whiteflies
    C. Neem oil 5 ml/liter spray repels whiteflies
    D. Imidacloprid seed treatment provides early-season protection
    E. Do not use cotton varieties that are CLCuV susceptible 
       (avoid unknown/uncertified varieties in Pakistan)
  - Pakistan CLCuV is a major problem in Punjab — use resistant varieties 
    (IUB-222, FH-142, NIAB-512, CIM-598)

3.7 FOLIAR NUTRITION SPRAY PROGRAM (Preventive)
-------------------------------------------------
  Apply as foliar spray package at bud initiation and repeat at 21 days:
    - Magnesium Sulfate: 2% (20 g/liter)
    - Zinc Sulfate: 0.5% (5 g/liter)
    - Urea: 1% (10 g/liter) — only if nitrogen needed
    - Boric acid: 0.1% (1 g/liter) — boron improves nutrient uptake

  Apply in the evening (after 5 PM) — prevents leaf burn in hot weather
  Strain spray solution through fine cloth before use to avoid nozzle clog
